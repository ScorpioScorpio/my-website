Immigrant-rights groups, including the ACLU, are challenging this in federal court, and it has not been settled. Until there's a definitive ruling, the safest practice remains: don't open the door unless a judicial warrant, signed by a judge and naming your address, is shown. If agents enter anyway, don't physically resist — stay calm, comply physically, and say clearly, "I do not consent to this entry."

Every person, regardless of status

You have constitutional rights.

These rights apply to everyone in the United States, no matter your immigration status. Practicing what to say before an encounter makes it much easier to remember in the moment.

RIGHT · 01

Remain silent

You do not have to answer questions about where you were born, your status, or how you entered the country.

"I am exercising my right to remain silent."
RIGHT · 02

Refuse a search

You can decline to let agents search your home, car, phone, or belongings if they don't have a valid judicial warrant.

"I do not consent to a search."
RIGHT · 03

Not open the door

You don't have to open your door unless agents show a warrant signed by a judge. Ask them to slide it under the door.

"Please slide the warrant under the door."
RIGHT · 04

Speak to a lawyer

If detained or arrested, you can ask for a lawyer immediately and stay silent until one is present.

"I want to speak to a lawyer."
RIGHT · 05

Decline an interview

You can refuse a voluntary interview even if agents say you aren't under arrest.

"I don't wish to answer questions right now."
RIGHT · 06

Never show false documents

Don't lie or claim false citizenship — this can seriously harm your case. Staying silent is always safer than lying.

Say nothing, rather than something false.
The legal limits on enforcement

What ICE is — and isn't — allowed to do.

The single most important thing to check in any encounter is the kind of warrant an agent is holding. The two look similar but mean very different things.

Judicial warrant

Signed by a judge

  • Says "U.S. District Court" or a state court at the top
  • Signed by a judge or magistrate
  • Names a specific person and address
  • This is what's required to enter your home or a private area without permission
Administrative warrant

Form I-200 or I-205

  • Says "Department of Homeland Security," not a court
  • Signed by an ICE official, not a judge
  • Is a valid basis to arrest someone in public
  • Does not, by itself, allow agents to enter your home without consent

WORKPLACE

Public vs. private areas

ICE can enter areas of a business open to the public without a warrant, but needs a judicial warrant or the employer's consent to enter non-public areas, like a kitchen or back office.

VEHICLE STOPS

Pulled over

Pull over safely, turn off the engine and interior light on at night. You must show license, registration, and insurance if asked — but you can decline a search of the vehicle.

RECORDING

Filming in public

If you're not under arrest, you generally have the right to photograph or film ICE activity in public, as long as you don't physically interfere.

Do this before you need it

Prepare your family now.

A plan made calmly, ahead of time, is far more useful than trying to remember one during a frightening moment. Go through this list with everyone in your household, including roommates and children old enough to understand.

Memorize a lawyer's phone number — write it on paper too, in case a phone is taken. Add it to every household member's contacts.

Choose a trusted emergency contact — someone who can pick up your kids, care for pets, or access money if you're suddenly detained.

Consider a caregiver's authorization or power of attorney for your children, so a trusted adult can make decisions for them if you're not reachable. A local nonprofit legal aid office can help you set this up for free.

Keep key documents together and accessible — passports, birth certificates, immigration paperwork, medical and school records — either in one place or copies with your trusted contact.

Memorize your A-number (if you have one, found on immigration documents) — it helps a lawyer or family member locate you if you're detained.

Practice your rights out loud with your household — what to say, and just as importantly, what not to sign or say.

Post a "no consent to enter" notice near your door in the languages your household speaks, and know that it's not a barrier by itself — you still need to say it aloud.

Never sign anything you don't fully understand without a lawyer present — including anything presented as routine paperwork.

Save a hotline number in your phone under a name you'll recognize in a hurry — see the hotlines below.

Look up your consulate's phone number — you have the right to contact your country's consulate if detained.
